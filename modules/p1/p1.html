How does feedback make a system faster and more accurate?

A PID controller compares the setpoint with the output and pushes the plant harder when it lags behind. Change Kp, Ki and Kd and watch rise time, overshoot, settling time and steady-state error change.

Example: the Module 1 plant, made ten times faster

Take K = 1 and τ = 1 s. On its own the plant needs 3τ = 3 s to reach 95% of a step. Close the loop with a P controller, Kp = 9:

  1. τcl0.1 sτ / (1 + KKp) = 1 / 10, ten times faster
  2. 95% point0.3 s3τcl instead of 3 s
  3. ess10%1 / (1 + KKp): it settles at 0.9, short of 1

Why 10% short? P only pushes while there is an error: to hold y = 0.9 it needs u = 0.9 = 9e, so e stays at 0.1. Adding I removes that error.

      What P, I and D do

      Typical trends when one gain is raised; the chart always has the final word.

      P: push in proportion to the error

      A larger Kp gives a faster rise and a smaller steady-state error, 1/(1 + KKp), but never zero. On the second-order plant it also increases the overshoot.

      I: remove the remaining error

      Ki∫e keeps growing while any error is left, so the loop only rests at e = 0: zero steady-state error for a step. Too much Ki adds overshoot and oscillation.

      D: react to the trend

      Kd de/dt acts like a brake when the output moves fast, reducing overshoot on the second-order plant. It does not change the steady-state error. (The derivative is filtered with Tf = 0.01 s.)

      Actuator limits

      When the PID asks for more than umax, the plant only gets umax (red shading). The response is then slower than the formulas predict, and the integral can wind up and add overshoot.
