The controller only controls what the sensor says

In P1 the controller saw the true output y. Real loops measure y with a sensor, and every sensor from Homework 1 has a time constant. Here a first-order sensor sits in the feedback path: the controller acts on its reading m(t), which lags behind y(t).

  1. τs = 0.01 s16%overshoot of y: the sensor is invisible
  2. τs = 0.3 s87%overshoot of y, but m shows only 60%
  3. τs ≥ 0.67 sunstablethe oscillation grows instead of dying out

Why? The thermometer reports the temperature late, so the controller keeps heating after the water is already hot enough, then keeps cooling after it is already cold enough. The gains did not change; the information did.

        Why a slow sensor causes overshoot and oscillation

        The controller responds to the measurement

        The PID computes e = r − m. It has no other information about the plant. If m is wrong or late, the controller acts on a wrong or late picture of the world, however good its gains are.

        A slow sensor reports changes late

        The sensor is the first-order system of Module 1: after a change it needs about τs to show 63% of it and 3τs to show 95%. While y is rising fast, m is still well below it, so the error looks bigger than it really is.

        The controller keeps over-correcting

        Because the reported error is too large, u stays high after y has already reached the setpoint, and the integral keeps accumulating error that is no longer there. y overshoots. On the way down the same thing happens in reverse. This is why the true y overshoots more than m shows.

        Delay eats stability

        The sensor adds a pole to the loop and phase lag at every frequency. With integral action the loop becomes third order and, beyond a certain τs, the oscillation grows instead of decaying. The exact limit depends on the plant and the gains, so a slower sensor does not always mean instability: a P-only loop on the first-order plant stays stable for any τs, it just rings more.

        What to do about it

        Check the sensor before blaming the gains: choose a sensor with τs well below the closed-loop time scale (a common rule of thumb is 5 to 10 times faster). If the sensor cannot be changed, lower Ki or Kp, or add some D (here acting on the reading), and accept a slower loop.

        The sensor hides the problem

        A slow sensor smooths what it reports. If you only looked at the logged measurement you would see a milder overshoot than the plant really had. On real hardware that could mean a motor or heater going further than the data suggests.
