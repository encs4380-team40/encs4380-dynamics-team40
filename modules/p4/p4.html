A useful tuning strategy

Start with a moderate proportional gain, then add integral action to remove steady-state error. If the response overshoots, reduce the gains or add a little derivative action.

  1. Step 1: PSpeedIncrease Kp until the rise is fast enough
  2. Step 2: IAccuracyUse Ki to reduce the final error
  3. Step 3: DDampingAdjust Kd to reduce excessive overshoot

      How to tune for the specifications

      A fast response is not automatically a good response.

      Rise time and settling time are different

      A response can rise quickly and then oscillate for a long time. The settling-time target asks when the response enters and stays within the ±2% final-value band.

      P alone usually leaves error

      In a first-order plant a proportional-only controller settles short of a step. Integral action is normally needed to meet the nearly-zero-error requirement.

      More gain is not always better

      High gains can saturate the actuator or destabilize the loop. Adjust the gains one at a time and compare the pinned curve before submitting your tuning.

      Use D to limit overshoot

      Derivative action resists rapid changes and can add effective damping. Since it does not remove steady-state error, use it alongside a suitable integral term.

      Challenge models use the same first-order and second-order equations as Homework 1, with random parameters. The hidden plant is revealed after scoring so you can compare the response with the physical model. P4 uses derivative-on-measurement to avoid a setpoint derivative kick; P1 and P3 use derivative-on-error in their default examples.
